01 — Overview

What this tool does

Aladdin Finance BI answers one question: over a given tenure, is it cheaper to lease a property (paying a large refundable deposit instead of monthly rent) or to rent it the conventional way?

In India's residential and commercial property market, the lease model — also called the pagdi or security deposit model in some regions — is widespread. A tenant pays a large lump sum upfront (the lease deposit) to the landlord in lieu of monthly rent. The deposit is returned at the end of the term. Under a rental agreement, the tenant pays monthly rent plus a much smaller deposit (typically 2–10 months' rent).

At first glance, leasing looks free — no monthly payments. But that capital is locked up for years, earning nothing. The real question is: what does that lock-up cost you? And does it cost more or less than paying monthly rent over the same period?

The tool makes that comparison rigorous, accounting for:

  • 💰EMI costs — if you borrowed to fund the deposit, what are you repaying each month, and what interest does that add up to?
  • 📈Opportunity cost — what could your locked capital have earned if invested instead? This is the single biggest hidden cost of leasing.
  • 📉Rent escalation — monthly rent grows every year; the lease deposit stays fixed. Over time, rent compounds against you.
  • ⚠️Withholding risk — landlords sometimes withhold part of the deposit at exit. That loss is a real cost.
  • 🔒Liquidity risk — a large locked deposit is illiquid; that illiquidity has a price.
  • 🚪Flexibility value — a rental gives you the right to leave with short notice; a lease doesn't.
Who this is for Individuals evaluating a residential lease deposit, retail or commercial business owners assessing a shop or office lease, and finance teams modelling a property strategy across multiple scenarios. The tool is built for the Indian market — all figures in INR — but the underlying model applies anywhere these structures exist.
02 — The Model

The financial model under the hood

Both the lease and rent paths are converted to a single comparable number: their economic monthly cost over the lease term.

Lease cost

The total economic cost of leasing over a term of N months is:

// Lease total economic cost Total Lease Cost = Total EMI Interest Paid // real cash, interest portion only + Withholding Loss // deposit × withholding % + Own Funds Opportunity Cost // compounded over term + Post-Loan Opportunity Cost // tail months after loan repaid + Refund Delay Cost // delay months × opp rate + Total Maintenance // monthly maint × N + Risk Cost // deposit × risk % × (N/12) Monthly Lease Cost = Total Lease Cost / N

The opportunity cost is computed in two phases. During the loan repayment period, only own funds (deposit minus loan) are truly your locked capital — the borrowed portion was never yours to invest. After the loan is repaid, the full deposit is effectively your capital, so the full deposit bears opportunity cost for the tail months. This is a more accurate model than simply applying the opportunity rate to the entire deposit for the full term.

Rent cost

// Rent total economic cost over same term Total Rent Cost = Σ Monthly Rent × (1 + growth)^year // escalating annually + Rent Deposit Opportunity Cost // compounded over term + Rent Deposit Refund Delay Cost // delay penalty − Flexibility Premium × N // option value of exit rights Monthly Rent Cost = Total Rent Cost / N

The verdict

Monthly Saving = Monthly Rent Cost − Monthly Lease Cost Score (0–100) = 50 + 50 × (saving fraction) // >50 = lease wins Confidence = |saving fraction| × 100 // margin of decision

Opportunity cost rate

The model uses a certainty-equivalent real opportunity rate:

Real Rate = (1 + nominal/12) / (1 + inflation/12) − 1 // monthly Vol Penalty = 0.5 × σ² / 12 // Kelly-style certainty-equivalent haircut CE Rate = max(0, Real Rate − Vol Penalty) + Liquidity Premium/12

If your alternative investment is volatile (e.g. equity mutual funds), you can't count on hitting the quoted return with certainty. The volatility penalty discounts the rate accordingly. A high-volatility portfolio thus makes the locked lease deposit look slightly less costly — because your investable alternative is less reliable.

Important distinction The model computes economic cost, not just cash outflow. Economic cost includes opportunity cost on locked capital — money you don't actually pay out, but lose nonetheless. The dashboard shows both separately: "cash outflows" (EMI + maintenance) and "full economic cost" (including the invisible opportunity cost).
03 — Quick Start

Getting started in 5 steps

You can be looking at a result in under two minutes. Here's the fastest path.

  1. Load a preset that matches your situation. Click one of the four quick-scenario buttons at the top: Metro Apartment, High-Street Retail, Office Space, or Warehouse. This populates all fields with realistic defaults for that property type — a good baseline to modify from.
  2. Enter your actual lease deposit amount. This is the single most important number. It's the total deposit the landlord is asking for — not the property value. Enter it in the Lease Deposit (₹) field.
  3. Set the loan amount. How much of that deposit are you borrowing? Enter it in Loan Against Deposit. If you're self-funding, enter 0. The difference is your own funds locked — watch the orange badge update.
  4. Enter the equivalent market rent. What would you pay monthly if you rented the same property instead? This is the key comparison number for the rent side.
  5. Read the gauge and verdict banner. The circular gauge shows the decision score (0–100). Above 50 = lease wins. The banner gives you the monthly saving and full-term impact. Scroll down for charts and confidence analysis.
Pro tip Use the ⓘ buttons next to each field for detailed guidance on what to enter for your specific property type — residential, retail, office, or warehouse. Each field has 4–6 sections of context-specific help.
04 — Input Reference

Every input, explained

There are three groups of inputs: Lease Components, Rent Components, and Economic Assumptions. All have slider + number input pairs that stay in sync.

Lease Components

Parameter Typical Range What it means
Lease Deposit (₹) ₹5L – ₹2Cr Total upfront deposit paid to the landlord. Do not enter the property's market value. This is only the deposit sum — loan + own funds.
Loan Against Deposit (₹) ₹0 – ≤ deposit Borrowed portion of the deposit (personal loan, business OD, LAP). Enter 0 if fully self-funded. Own funds = deposit − loan.
Interest Rate (%) 7% – 22% Annual interest rate on the loan. The model uses a standard reducing-balance EMI formula. Typical ranges: top-up home loan 8.5–9.5%, personal loan 10–14%, business OD 12–18%.
Maintenance (₹/mo) ₹0 – ₹50K Monthly costs to keep the property operational — society charges, AMC, minor repairs. Exclude utilities. Residential: ₹2K–₹25K; Retail/Office: ₹5K–₹50K+.
Withholding (%) 0% – 30% Percentage of the deposit the landlord withholds at exit (for repairs, defaults, fit-out restoration). Registered leases with reputed landlords: 0–5%. Informal high-value deposits: up to 15%.
Refund Delay (months) 0 – 6 Months after the lease ends before the deposit is returned. Even a 1-month delay on a large deposit has real opportunity cost. Model charges opportunity cost for this period on the refundable amount.

Rent Components

Parameter Typical Range What it means
Market Rent (₹/mo) ₹5K – ₹5L What you would pay monthly if renting the same property. This is the rent side's primary cost driver. Use 2–3 comparable market quotes.
Rent Deposit (₹) ₹0 – ₹20L Security deposit under a rental agreement. Bengaluru: 5–10 months rent. Mumbai/Delhi: 2–3 months. Model charges opportunity cost on this amount for the full comparison period.
Deposit Refund Delay (months) 0 – 6 Months before the rent deposit is returned. Mirrors the lease-side refund delay parameter.
Rent Growth (%/year) 0% – 15% Annual rent escalation. Applied as a step function every 12 months. Metro residential: 5–10%. Prime retail/industrial: 6–12%. This is the strongest lever in favour of leasing over long tenures.
Exit Likelihood Low / Possible / Likely How likely you are to need to exit early. Sets the flexibility premium (0 / 6% / 16% of market rent per month) that is subtracted from the rent side's total cost, representing the option value of rent's flexibility.

Tenure Settings

Parameter Typical Range What it means
Lease Tenure (months) 6 – 120 How long you are committed to the property. Sets the comparison horizon. All costs compound over this period. Residential: 11–36 months; Retail/Office: 36–60 months; Warehouse: 36–84 months.
Loan Tenure (months) 6 – lease tenure Repayment period for the loan. Capped automatically at lease tenure. A shorter loan tenure means EMI-free months at the end — which significantly improves lease economics. E.g. 36-month lease with 24-month loan = 12 EMI-free months.

Economic Assumptions

Parameter Typical Range What it means
Opportunity Cost Rate (%) 7% – 30% What your locked capital could earn in its next-best use. FD/PPF: 7–8%. Balanced fund: 10–13%. Equity index: 13–16%. Business capital: 18–30%. Use your actual expected return, not an aspirational number.
Net Lease Risk Premium (%) 0.5% – 6% Extra cost for risks unique to leasing vs renting — deposit recovery risk, lock-in risk, counterparty risk. Institutional landlord with registered lease: 0.5–1%. Individual lessor, informal arrangement: 2–5%.
Inflation Rate (%) 3% – 9% Expected annual CPI. Used to convert the nominal opportunity rate to a real rate. Does not escalate rent (that's controlled by Rent Growth). RBI target: 4–5%. Recent urban average: 5.5–7%.
Liquidity Premium (%) 0.5% – 4% Extra annual return required to compensate for the illiquidity of a years-long deposit lock-up, above a short-term rent deposit. Higher if the deposit is a large fraction of your liquid wealth.
Return Volatility (%) 3% – 40% How volatile your alternative investment returns are. High volatility → lower certainty-equivalent opportunity rate → leasing looks slightly more attractive. Nifty 50: ~16–18%. FD: 3–5%. Business capital: 25–40%.
Most common input error Entering the property's market value in the Lease Deposit field instead of the actual deposit amount. If a flat is worth ₹80L and the landlord demands ₹20L deposit, enter ₹20L — not ₹80L. Entering ₹80L overstates all costs by 4× and produces completely wrong results.

06 — Outputs

Reading the outputs

The dashboard shows results in five layers: a score gauge, a verdict banner, key metrics, detailed charts, and analytical deep-dives. Here's what each means.

The score gauge (0–100)

The circular gauge is the headline verdict. It maps the lease advantage fraction to a 0–100 scale anchored at 50.

85 – 100 STRONG LEASE — The numbers clearly favour leasing. Proceed with confidence unless qualitative factors are strongly against it.
65 – 84 LEASE AHEAD — Meaningful economic edge. Check qualitative factors (stability, landlord risk) to confirm.
50 – 64 LEASE EDGE — Marginal advantage. Qualitative factors may tip the decision either way.
35 – 49 RENT EDGE — Renting has a slight economic edge. Consider whether non-financial factors justify leasing.
0 – 34 RENT WINS — Renting is significantly more economical. Strong financial case against leasing.

Key metrics row

MetricWhat it tells you
Monthly EMI Your loan repayment per month. This is real cash leaving your account.
Maintenance Monthly upkeep cost — also real cash. EMI + Maintenance = your actual monthly outflow under the lease.
Monthly / Annual / Term Gain Economic saving (positive = lease wins) on a monthly, annual, and full-term basis. These are economic figures, not cash savings — they include opportunity cost.
Effective Cost / Mo The full economic monthly cost of the lease (after amortising all costs including opportunity cost). Compare this to market rent to gauge the economic premium or saving.
Cash Payback Period How many months of cash savings (market rent minus EMI and maintenance) it takes to recover your own funds. This is a pure cash metric — excludes opportunity cost.
Confidence How decisive the margin is, as a percentage. High confidence = the result is robust to moderate input changes. Low confidence = a small shift in assumptions reverses the verdict.

Decision Confidence & Breakeven Thresholds

This card answers: how close is this to a coin flip? It shows three breakeven thresholds — the value of market rent, loan rate, and rent growth at which leasing and renting become exactly equal. If your current inputs are far from all three thresholds, the verdict is stable. If you're close to any one threshold, a small real-world change could flip the decision.

Implied Return Threshold (IRT)

The IRT is the internal rate of return (IRR) that the lease arrangement is implicitly generating on your incremental capital versus renting. It answers: "If I put extra capital into this lease deposit instead of renting, what return does that capital earn?" Compare it to your opportunity cost rate — if IRT exceeds your opportunity rate, the lease is working harder than your investment alternative. If not, your capital would do better elsewhere.

Conflict signal If the monthly cost model favours leasing but the IRT is below your opportunity rate, the tool flags a conflict. This happens when the lease saves you money on a monthly basis but the incremental capital deployed generates a sub-par return. The right choice depends on whether you would actually invest the monthly premium at your stated opportunity rate.

08 — Advanced Features

Beyond the basics: advanced tools

Scenario Analysis Grid

The 3×3 scenario grid stress-tests the decision across nine combinations of market rent (−15%, base, +15%) and rent growth (−3pp, base, +3pp). Green cells = lease wins in that scenario. Red = rent wins. Use this to understand whether the verdict is robust to market uncertainty — if most cells are green, you have a stable lease advantage. If it's mixed, the decision is sensitive to market direction.

Early Exit Analysis

Drag the slider to simulate leaving before the lease term ends. The tool shows your cash paid out, net deposit recovery (deposit refund minus outstanding loan balance — which can be negative in early months if the outstanding loan exceeds the refund), cumulative rent you would have paid instead, and your net position versus renting at that exit month. This is critical for assessing lock-in risk.

Key insight In the first few months of a lease, the net position vs. renting is almost always negative — you've paid EMI and maintenance but your deposit isn't generating savings yet. The chart shows when the cumulative lease position turns positive. If that month is well within the lease term, exit risk is manageable.

Qualitative Checklist

Six non-financial factors that can override a marginal financial verdict. Check each that applies to your situation. The tool gives a qualitative score and a combined assessment. A 4/6 or higher qualitative score generally reinforces a marginal financial advantage into a confident recommendation. A 0–2/6 qualitative score may justify renting even if the financial numbers marginally favour the lease.

URL Sharing

Click 📤 Share to copy a URL with all your current inputs encoded as parameters. Send it to a colleague or advisor to review the same scenario. The URL includes every slider value — the recipient sees exactly your numbers.

Light/Dark Theme

Toggle between dark (default) and light mode using the button in the top right. The preference is saved in your browser's local storage.

09 — Common Mistakes

Mistakes that break the model

  • ❌ Entering property market value as the lease deposit. If the flat is worth ₹1Cr and the landlord asks ₹20L deposit, enter ₹20L — not ₹1Cr. This is the most common error and overstates lease costs by 3–10×.
  • ❌ Using an aspirational opportunity rate instead of a realistic one. "I could earn 25% in the market" is not an opportunity cost unless you would actually invest the money in something generating 25%. Use your actual blended portfolio return. If you keep savings in FDs and liquid funds, 8–9% is more honest than 18%.
  • ❌ Ignoring rent growth. Entering 0% for rent growth makes renting look artificially cheap. Metro residential rents have grown 7–15% annually in 2022–2024. Even a conservative 5–6% growth dramatically changes the 5-year comparison.
  • ❌ Setting flexibility premium at zero when exit risk is real. If there's a genuine chance you'll need to exit early — business risk, relocation, life change — that option has value. Leaving it at zero overstates the lease's advantage.
  • ❌ Setting loan tenure longer than the lease tenure. The tool auto-caps this, but consciously: if your repayment extends beyond when you'll vacate, you'd still owe principal at exit. The early exit simulator shows this as negative net deposit recovery.
  • ❌ Ignoring the maintenance field. Monthly society charges, AMC contracts, and minor repair budgets are real costs under the lease that have no equivalent under renting (where the landlord bears structural maintenance). Leaving it at zero understates the lease's true cost.
  • ❌ Comparing a lease deposit to co-working without adjusting the rent deposit. Co-working typically requires zero deposit (or 1 month advance). If renting means co-working, set the rent deposit to near zero and set the flexibility premium to zero (flexibility is already priced into the co-working model).

The collapsible sections

The three input groups — Lease Components, Rent Components, and Economic Assumptions — can each be collapsed by clicking their section header. This is useful on small screens or when you want to focus on one group at a time.

Validation banners

The tool shows two types of banners above the inputs: red error banners block the calculation (e.g. loan exceeds deposit, tenure too short) and yellow warning banners flag unusual values that are allowed but worth reviewing (e.g. opportunity rate above 35%, rent growth above 15%). Resolve red errors before reading results.

The direction indicators

The ▲ ▼ ● indicators next to Monthly Gain, Annual Gain, and Term Gain provide colour-blind-safe signals. ▲ = lease is winning. ▼ = rent is winning. ● = too close to call.
